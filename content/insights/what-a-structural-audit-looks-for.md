People usually call about a structural audit for one of four reasons. Cracks have
appeared. They want to add a floor. They are buying the building. Or a previous
owner removed something and nobody knows whether it mattered.

Here is roughly what the assessment covers, and what the findings mean.

## Cracks: not all equal

The useful questions about a crack are where it is, which way it runs, how wide
it is, and whether it is still moving.

Fine hairline cracking in plaster is usually shrinkage and cosmetic. Diagonal
cracks running through structural walls, particularly ones that widen toward the
top or bottom, suggest differential settlement, meaning parts of the building are
moving relative to each other. Cracks that follow the line of a beam or column,
or that appear at the same position on several floors, are worth taking seriously
immediately.

Whether a crack is still moving matters more than how wide it is. A wide crack
that stopped moving ten years ago is a different problem from a narrow one that
opened last month.

## Where the loads actually go

The assessment traces the load path: how weight travels from roof to slabs to
beams to columns to foundations to ground. Buildings fail where that path has been
interrupted.

The most common interruption is a wall that was removed during a renovation. In
many buildings, particularly older or unsupervised ones, walls that look like
partitions are carrying load. Removing one transfers that load somewhere it was
not designed to go, and the building tells you about it slowly.

The second most common is a floor that was added. A structure designed for two
storeys and carrying four is not necessarily failing today, but its margin is
gone.

## Condition of the concrete and steel

Reinforced concrete fails from the inside out. Water gets in, reaches the steel,
the steel rusts, rust expands, and the expansion spalls the concrete off from
within. By the time you can see rust stains and flaking on a soffit or column,
the process has been running for a while.

The assessment looks at cover to reinforcement, signs of carbonation, spalling,
and anywhere water has been sitting against structure for years: parapets, slab
edges, balconies, planter boxes, and wet areas with failed waterproofing.

## Water, again

Persistent damp is a structural issue, not a decorating issue. It corrodes
reinforcement, degrades mortar, softens the ground under foundations, and it is
almost always caused by something ordinary: a blocked downpipe, a failed roof
detail, ground levels raised above the damp course, or drainage that sends water
toward the building rather than away.

## Reading the report

A useful audit report separates urgent from important from cosmetic, and says
what happens if you do nothing. Findings should come with a prioritised schedule
and indicative costs, because "the building has issues" is not actionable and
"everything must be fixed immediately" is usually not true.

The honest outcome of many audits is that the building is fine, the cracks are
cosmetic, and the report says so in two pages. That is a legitimate result and
you should be suspicious of a consultant who never produces one.
